02 · Origin

A Mississippi kid
who built a life
in Texas.

Below, the through line behind two decades of building companies, raising a family, and finding home in Canyon Lake.

I grew up in Madison, Mississippi. Small town, big family table, the kind of place that taught you early what it looks like when somebody means what they say. That's the part I never let go of, no matter how far the work has taken me.

My first business was a restaurant I started with my grandmother in 2005. Working a kitchen taught me more about leadership in two years than most of the books I've read since. You don't fake your way through a Saturday night dinner rush. The food either goes out or it doesn't, and the people across the counter know the difference.

In 2007 I started a party rental company, grew it for several years, and eventually sold it to a competitor. I stayed on as general manager under a four-year contract afterward and used that runway to learn the unglamorous parts of the work. Payroll, fleet, scheduling, contracts, and the daily math of running a real operation. Most of what I know about leading people I learned in those years. None of it was theoretical.

From there I moved into commercial contracting. We did new-model build-outs and assembly-line maintenance for the Nissan plant. Anytime they changed over their assembly line, my crews bid the work. Painting, integration, install, all kinds of commercial trades inside the plant. Continental Tire was the same kind of relationship. McAlister's and Cinnabon were nationwide digital signage rollouts. The state DMV facilities were where it got interesting, because we ended up automating their ticketing systems. That's the same thread I'm still pulling on today, just with better tools.

Around the same window I built and ran a vending company with more than 1,500 machines deployed across the Southeast, from Texas through Florida. Logistics, route management, restock, repair, and a whole lot of late nights in trucks. It was its own master class in operations.

That's the gap I work in now. Through PointWake, I design and deploy multi-agent Gemini, Claude, and OpenAI systems for property management, roofing, and emergency-service operators. Work gets split into modular, reusable agents — Researcher, Builder, Orchestrator — that share state through structured JSON contracts so we can swap the underlying model and tooling without rebuilding the workflow.

Every engagement starts the same way. Map the workflow in SIPOC or IGOE form. Quantify the baseline. Define the metrics that actually matter — cost per unit, cycle time, throughput, lead quality score. Then, and only then, decide whether a human, an automation, or an agent should own the next step. Operations First. Automation Second. AI Last. It's why my agents tend to survive contact with production.

On the model-behavior side, I work as a contract prompt engineer and RLHF specialist on Mercor's Project Balboa and with Handshake AI — ranking frontier-model outputs, writing reference completions, and labeling failure modes that feed directly into fine-tuning pipelines. That gives me a working understanding of where models hallucinate, where instruction-following breaks down, and how to enforce data boundaries in prompts and tool definitions. I completed the Post Graduate Program in Generative AI for Business Applications at the McCombs School of Business at the University of Texas at Austin in May 2026.

But the through line was never the work. It was always the life behind the work. I met my wife, Audriana, and we built our family in the Texas Hill Country. We raise our boy, Rowdy, in Canyon Lake. On the water as often as the calendar lets us. Around the dinner table the rest of the time. Everything I build is in service of that.

I've made just about every mistake a young operator can make. I've also fixed most of them. That's why I do this work. And why every page of this site is honest about who's behind it.

Methodology

Operations First. Automation Second. AI Last.

Most generative AI pilots fail because they layer models onto broken processes. I do it the other way. Workflows get mapped (SIPOC / IGOE), instrumented, and stabilized first. Then we automate the parts that can be deterministic. Then — and only then — do we let a model touch the work, with a RAG layer that grounds it in your data and a human-in-the-loop checkpoint on anything irreversible.

The same operating playbook that makes a company ready for AI is the playbook that makes it ready for due diligence.

What I believe

Four principles that drive the work.

These aren't slogans. They're how I run companies and how I try to live.

01
Family, faith, work. In that order.
The order is the test. When the order changes, the rest stops working. Everything I build is downstream of that priority.
02
Lead with your voice. Mean what you say.
Both halves matter. Silence isn't humility, and talk without follow through is just noise. The standards you set in conversation become the standards your team sets in their work.
03
Documentation is leverage.
If a process can't be written down, it can't be trained, automated, sold, or exited. Doing the work is half of it. Writing it down is the other half. The half that compounds.
04
You get out what you put in.
No shortcuts that aren't actually paid for somewhere else. The line my dad lived by, and the one I try to leave with my son.
